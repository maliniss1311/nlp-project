5. Analysis Questions
----------------------

Q1: Why can a trigram model assign probability zero to a reasonable
    phrase that was not observed during training?

A trigram model estimates P(w3|w1,w2) directly from counts:
C(w1,w2,w3) / C(w1,w2). If the specific three-word sequence was never
seen in training, the count C(w1,w2,w3) = 0, making the probability
exactly zero — regardless of whether the phrase is linguistically
reasonable. The model has no mechanism to generalize beyond what it has
explicitly observed. The space of possible trigrams grows as V^3, so
even a large training corpus will leave most valid trigrams unseen.


Q2: Why does taking log2(0) cause a problem when calculating perplexity?

log2(0) is mathematically undefined (it tends to negative infinity). In
the perplexity formula, we sum log2 P(wi|context) over all test tokens.
A single zero-probability token sends the entire sum to negative
infinity, making the perplexity 2^(+infinity) = infinity. This means
one unseen trigram can make the model appear infinitely bad, even if it
predicts every other word well. This is why smoothing techniques or a
probability floor are essential — they ensure no event receives exactly
zero probability.


Q3: How can Word2Vec identify words related to "tannins" even when a
    particular word sequence never appeared in the training corpus?

Word2Vec learns dense vector representations by training on local
context windows, not on specific n-gram sequences. Words that frequently
appear in similar contexts (e.g., surrounded by words like "firm",
"dry", "astringent", "oak") are pushed close together in the embedding
space — even if they never co-occur directly. So "tannins" and a
related word like "succulent" might both appear near wine-structure
descriptors, giving them similar vectors. This distributional similarity
("you shall know a word by the company it keeps" — Firth, 1957) allows
Word2Vec to capture semantic relationships that count-based n-gram
models completely miss.
